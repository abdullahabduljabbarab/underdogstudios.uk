Sound and effects

Built and playtested; licences and voice limits open

Sound and effects tell players what is happening before they have time to look: a courier should hear a cop closing in, and a cop should see the van brake before it turns. Until late October the cars were completely silent, so this work filled a gap in one of the game's core ideas, that sound is a warning.

Every sound fades with one shared rule: full volume within 10 m, gone by 90 m. That is close to how far a cop can see. The siren (see the police chapter) carries much further.

Behind the design

Why sound

Built 25 to 27 Oct 2026 · Who Abdullah (recordings, every call by ear, code), Xabi (replacement sounds to come)

Inspired by

Collins (2008) shows that game sound does more than set a mood: it tells the player what the game is doing and what they should react to.

What we took: every car sound carries information, such as which car is near, how hard a hit was and whether an engine is dying. What we changed: we kept the information and dropped realism where it got in the way, with fake gears and exaggerated landings that suit an arcade game.

What we set out to do

Bring back the driving effects the prototype had, so they show on every screen, and give each car a voice players can tell apart by ear. Every sound had to be swappable in the editor, so Xabi can replace it later without code.

How it was built

Effects first. The lean, smoke, skid marks and lights came first. Real physics lean was tried in the prototype and could not pass about 5 degrees, so the lean is drawn on a copy of the body that never collides. It can be as big as the moment needs and can never tip the car over. Abdullah asked for the drift smoke to be "more arcadey and exaggerated", so it became bigger, whiter and slower to clear. For the exhaust effects he chose nitro flames and backfires before dirt dust, because players see them far more often.

The driver's pedals and nitro are shared about 15 times a second, so every screen shows the same effects.

Sound. Abdullah noticed during the parcel work that the cars had been silent all along. He gathered the recordings, judged every sound by ear and steered about a dozen rounds of changes. The engine follows the car's speed through fake gears rather than the real engine revs, because the arcade handling pushes the car itself and the real revs did not match what the player felt.

The landings took the most time. Abdullah's notes over several rounds were that they had no weight, sounded synthetic, sounded like a tin can rather than a two tonne van, sounded like one sound instead of clanking, and were "too stable" instead of a chaotic mess. A real landing is many uneven hits at once, so each car got four takes built from real impact recordings, picked at random with a small change of pitch. Abdullah also asked for parcels thudding and sliding in the back when the van lands, so the cargo is heard as well as seen.

Where no good recording existed, such as the turbo whistle and the net launch, the sound was made for the game; recorded clips still need their licences checked.

Lessons

  • A loop shorter than about a second is heard as a rhythm, so engine loops need a long, steady stretch.
  • Kerbs and small bumps never leave the ground, so they have to be heard from the suspension, not from air time.
  • When feedback says "it sounds the same", the change was too small: take bigger steps and check the new file is the one playing.
