Under the hood

Networking

For the team, networking decides three things: whether a player's own car feels instant, whether a ram or a net catch looks the same on every screen, and whether a session can be hosted at all from a university lab. This chapter covers the model chosen, the settings that make it work, exactly what is sent, and what real online tests found.

Choosing the network model

Shady Shipping is a physics-driven car chase for up to five players. Contact between cars decides the match, so every screen must agree on it. Four established models were compared.

ModelStrengthWeaknessVerdict
Deterministic lockstepOnly inputs sent; tiny bandwidthNeeds identical physics on every PC; Chaos is notRejected
Server authority with snapshot interpolationSmooth, consistent remote carsRemote cars shown slightly in the past, so rams missKept for traffic only
Server authority with prediction and resimulationOwn car instant; server decides outcomesCorrections can show as a small hopChosen
Client-authoritative movementSimple and responsiveCheating; each side believes its own crashRejected

The chosen model works like this. Each player's game runs the car physics slightly ahead of the server, so their own car reacts the moment they press a key. Other players' cars are moved forward from the last input received for them, easing off towards no input. When the server's version of a car disagrees with the client's, the client rewinds to that physics frame and replays every frame since with the corrected data. That replay is called resimulation. Chaos, Unreal's physics engine, supports this natively in UE 5.8.

Because remote cars are predicted forward instead of shown in the past, a cop and the van meet at roughly the same moment on every screen. That suits the net and the arrest ring. The cost is that a sudden brake or swerve by a remote player makes the prediction briefly wrong, and the correction can show as a small hop that grows with latency (the delay for a message to reach the server). Server-side rewind, as used for instant-hit shots in shooters, was rejected because it is far more complex for vehicle physics.

The fixed physics step matters beyond networking. Anything that pushes a car (nitro, the net's rope, burst tyres, a garage driving the van) runs inside FSSArcadeVehicleSimulation on the physics thread. A force added from ordinary game code would not be replayed during resimulation, so the car would fight its own corrections.

Effect windows

Police tools change how a car drives, so they must land on the same physics frame everywhere. The server stores each effect on the car as an effect window: which effect, its strength and the server physics frames it covers. Every machine applies it on those frames, and a machine that receives it late rewinds to its first frame.

EffectCaused by
Snare and RopeThe bumper net
TyresA spike strip
SlowLights Out
WreckThe van or a cop reaching 0 HP
DriveA repair garage or the round's drive-in
DragTesting

Engine physics actions were tried first. At 150 ms latency only 1 of 7, then 8 of 13, applied, because an action arriving after its frame was stored was never used. Effect windows started on the server's frame on every machine at 0 to 200 ms with up to 5% packet loss, with the largest correction 15 cm. Nitro rides inside the car's own recorded input, in its unused yaw channel, so it replays with everything else.

Not replicated at all

Car sounds, skid marks, smoke, damage looks, cameras, the radar, route arrows, ability shakes and rumble, and the drive-in film are all worked out on each PC. The driver sends throttle, brake and boost state at most 15 times a second so other PCs can draw lights and flames; everything else comes from replicated movement.

Round start and loading

The round start follows the same rule as everything else: the server decides, each screen shows it. Two problems found in testing shaped it.

ProblemFix
A slow PC stalls for seconds after loading and missed the drive-inEach PC sends ServerLoadedIn after 15 smooth frames; the server waits for all, up to 90 s
A late phase update left a screen stuck on the countdownEach screen ends the countdown from its own synced clock; measured within 0.25 s of the server

Information the police must not have

The design hides the van from the police except through their radar. In the code, the courier's job and cargo tier never reach a police PC, and the police radar only draws what USSHeatComponent sends. However, every player car is always relevant (sent to every player whatever the distance; Epic Games, n.d.e), so the van's exact position does reach police PCs, and a modified client could read it. The original design limited the van to cops within 150 m. That was dropped because cars past the engine's 150 m cut-off vanished from other players' screens, and with at most five cars the cost of sending all of them is small. The team accepts the cheating risk for a university prototype.

Inspired by

Fiedler, Gaffer On Games networked physics series (2014a; 2014b; 2014c; 2015): compares deterministic lockstep, snapshot interpolation and state synchronisation for physics games, and shows what each costs in bandwidth and latency.

What we took: the comparison itself, and the idea of sending state plus inputs so remote objects can be simulated forward. What we changed: we use the engine's built-in resimulation instead of writing our own synchronisation.

Inspired by

Epic Games, Networked Physics Overview (n.d.b): describes Chaos physics prediction, where clients run ahead, keep a history of physics frames and resimulate when the server corrects them.

What we took: the Resimulation replication mode for every player car. What we changed: police tools are not sent as engine physics actions but as our own effect windows (see below), after testing showed late actions were dropped.

Inspired by

Fiedler, Fix Your Timestep! (2004): a simulation stepped at a fixed rate behaves the same however fast the game draws frames.

What we took: every force on a car lives in the fixed 60 Hz physics step. What we changed: nothing; the engine's async physics already works this way once it is switched on.

Inspired by

Aldridge, "I Shot You First: Networking the Gameplay of Halo: Reach" (2011): explains how Halo: Reach sorts what it sends into continuously updated state, one-off events and player control data, and gives priority to what matters most when bandwidth is short.

What we took: the same split. Phases and cash are state, cash pops and refusals are events, and car input is control data. What we changed: with only five players, every player car is always relevant instead of competing for priority.
